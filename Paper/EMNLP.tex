\begin{abstract}
Human--AI creative co-writing is increasingly popular for open ended text generation, yet most evaluations focus on final outputs rather than the interactional dynamics that produce the stories. We investigate narrative agency in turn-based co-writing across three settings: Human--Human, Human--AI, and AI--AI collaboration. Building on a previously introduced Human--AI corpus, we add Human--Human and AI--AI conditions under the same storytelling paradigm, enabling controlled comparison across pairings. We use an analytical framework that combines measures of affective alignment, semantic novelty, transience, and resonance to model how writers adapt to one another and shape subsequent narrative development. Our analysis shows that Human--AI co-writing constitutes a distinct asymmetric regime: LLMs accommodate human emotional tone and elaborate prior context, while humans introduce more novel and more persistent narrative material. In contrast, Human--Human and AI--AI interactions exhibit more symmetric patterns of adaptation. These findings suggest that LLMs function as adaptive amplifiers, both stabilizing and extending human-led narrative trajectories.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Human-AI creative co-writing is becoming a common mode of open-ended text generation \citep{noy2023experimental,clark-smith-2021-choose,cheng2025inspiration}, as human creative writers, professional or otherwise, integrate large language models (LLMs) into their workflows. Human writers tend to treat these systems, trained on vast textual corpora, as collaborators capable of contributing to narrative production \citep{lee2022coauthor, wan2024felt}. As such interactions become widespread, it becomes important to understand how these models participate in the unfolding, turn-by-turn dynamics of collaborative storytelling. 

Turn-based co-writing produces a controlled setting for studying human-LLM interaction as a dynamic process. In this setting, writing unfolds as a sequence of interdependent contributions: each turn conditions the next turn and may shift the story's semantic direction, emotional trajectory and subsequent context. Consequently, turn-based narrative generation makes it possible to track how emotional tone, semantic novelty and narrative influence move between writers over time. While work on human-LLM turn-based dynamics is a growing field, such studies often focus on final outputs, isolated agent contributions, or aggregated metrics, leaving less attention to the fine-grained interactional dynamics that construct a story.

In this work, we move from single-condition analysis to a comparative framework of collaborative dynamics. We test whether narrative agency and emotional alignment differ across three co-writing settings: Human-AI (HA), Human-Human (HH) and LLM-LLM (AA). Specifically, we examine how different pairings allocate three core functions of storytelling: (1) The emotional alignment between agents; (2) The generation of novel narrative material; (3) The adaptation to the evolving context of the story.

We use a turn-level analytical framework that models co-writing as a dynamic process of interactions, designed for both agents to have theoretical equal autonomy in the writing process. By modeling contributions at the level of individual turns, the framework measures affective alignment, semantic novelty, and directional influence, allowing us to trace how (human and artificial) writers respond to prior context and shape subsequent narrative development. 

This comparative perspective allows us to ask \textbf{whether the asymmetries observed in human-AI interaction reflect general features of collaborative writing}, or specific properties of human-LLM narrative coordination. 

We use this framework to investigate the following nuances of asymmetries:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Are dynamics of immediate emotional alignment between agents symmetric or asymmetric across conditions?}
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Are agent-level differences in novel contribution and narrative influence coordination a general property of dyadic co-writing or intrinsic for human-LLM interactions?}
    \item \textbf{RQ3: Do narrative coordination in human-AI stories resemble human-human collaboration, LLM-LLM collaboration, or a distinct regime?}
\end{enumerate}

Our contributions are threefold. First, building on an existing Human--AI co-writing corpus, we introduce two new datasets of Human--Human and AI--AI co-written narratives under the same turn-based storytelling paradigm as the existing corpus. Second, we use a turn-level framework to expand methodologies for measuring affective alignment, measured as immediate valence adaptation of each agent, and discourse influence, measured as the relationship between novelty-transience-resonance. Finally, we use our novel paradigm to show that human--AI co-writing forms distinct asymmetries in some narrative dynamics, but often exhibit similar, but amplified, patterns to human-human dynamics. AI-AI interactions, on the other hand, seem distinctively less asymmetric. Nonetheless, we observe that certain patterns are inherent for general dyadic storytelling across all conditions.

\section{Related Works}

\paragraph{Human-LLM co-creation}
Human--LLM co-writing is often motivated by the promise of hybrid intelligence: the possibility that human and artificial writers together may produce forms of creativity that neither could achieve alone \cite{cheng2025inspiration}. Much prior work on human–LLM creativity has evaluated the quality, originality, and productivity of aggregated outputs, finding mixed effects on creativity and ideation. 

\cite{Domanti_2026} compared human and LLM creativity by relating semantic network diversity to originality in idea-generation tasks, showing that LLM-generated outputs may be more original than those of low-creative humans in this context. In specific convergent and divergent thinking tasks, LLMs have also been found to outperform student participants  \cite{arora2025generative}. Furthermore, human-LLM co-creation may increase the number novel artifacts partly through the productivity effect \cite{zhou2025expands}, and has been associated with increased task enjoyment and improved writing quality \cite{noy2023experimental}. 

On the other hand, LLM outputs and human-LLM co-creation, may narrow the diversity of generated content, contributing to homogenization of ideas, semantic content, and stylistic profile \cite{wenger2025weredifferentweresame, anderson2024homogenization}. Human--LLM joint creativity may also reduce participants' self-percieved creativity \cite{luchini2025creative} and have found to underperfom human-human collaboration on creative outcomes \cite{tang2025best}.

We use the same turn-based storytelling paradigm across three dyadic conditions, to compare collaborative dynamics holding a constant task structure.\footnote{We will release our dataset and analysis framework upon acceptance} To the best of our knowledge, this is the first dataset that enables direct comparison of Human-Human, Human-AI and LLM-LLM turn-based co-writing under a shared experiment paradigm. 

The following instruction was provided to all agents across conditions:
\begin{quote}
\textit{“You are an author taking part in a collaborative storytelling activity with another author. Together, you will create a story by taking turns adding to it. Your goal is to continue from where your partner has left off. If there is no story, please begin the story. You have 10 interactions to write the story. Your input may be slightly truncated by a random number of characters.”}
\end{quote}

The resulting datasets consist of the following four levels of analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Token level (model level):} individual tokens $w$.
    \item \textbf{Turn level:} a single agent contribution at turn $t$, denoted $I_t$, which can be further specified as agent 1's turn ($A^{(1)}_t$) or agent 2's turn ($A^{(2)}_t$).
    \item \textbf{Interaction level:} paired contributions in the rolling dyadic exchange. Directional pairings are represented as $(A^{(1)}_t, A^{(2)}_t)$ when evaluating how agent 2 responds to agent 1's input, or as $(A^{(2)}_t, A^{(1)}_{t+1})$ when evaluating the reverse direction.
    \item \textbf{Story level:} a sequence of 10 interactions for session $i$, denoted $S_i$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods}
%Yb: perhaps a table here, for the metrics: metric, unit, tool, and subsection. 

We investigate co-writing dynamics through three dimensions: affective alignment, narrative influence (captured via transience and resonance) and surface level metrics.

We use a turn-level analytical framework that integrates embedding-based measures of semantic novelty and directional influence with sentiment-based measures of affective alignment.

\subsection{Valence}
For sentiment computation, we use concept vector projection, following the approach introduced by \cite{Lyngbaek_2026}. Given a precomputed concept vector $\hat{c}$ that encodes sentiment linearly, we get a continuous score of a turn $T_t$ by projecting its embedding $e_t$ onto the concept vector using the dot-product $e_t \cdot \hat{c}$ . 
The resulting valence score $v_t$ reflects the turn's ... along the negative-positive sentiment axis. Ranging from -1 to 1, a low score indicates a negative tone of the turn, whereas a high score indicates a positive tone. 

%Modelling
To model asymmetry between agents, we adopt an agent agnostic approach that evaluates unsigned asymmetry between partners. 

%Baseline differences
For baseline differences, we assess the absolute difference of mean valence scores between agents per story. This represents a measure of how far apart the two agents’ average valence baselines are within a story.

\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{baseline}}(S_i)
    =
    \left|
    \bar{v}^{(1)}_i
    -
    \bar{v}^{(2)}_i
    \right|
\end{equation}
where $\bar{v}^{(a)}_i$ is the mean valence of agent $a$ in story $S_i$.

Baseline asymmetry is consequently a measure of how much two paired agents differ in general sentiment. Two agents can have very high baseline asymmetry, with one being generally more positive than the other, while simultaneously showing high correlational local adaptation to each other -- referred to here as directional alignment.     

\subsection{Directional Alignment}
For asymmetries in directional alignment, we build on the framework from \cite{fundal2026directionalalignmentnarrativeagency} by regarding emotional adaptation as a relationship between predictor-turn valence and response-turn valence. For each story, we compute one alignment asymmetry magnitude as the absolute difference in correlation of directional alignment between agents.

\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{align}}(S_i)
    =
    \left|
    \operatorname{atanh}\!\left(r_i^{(1 \rightarrow 2)}\right)
    -
    \operatorname{atanh}\!\left(r_i^{(2 \rightarrow 1)}\right)
    \right|
\end{equation}
where $r_i^{(1 \rightarrow 2)}$ is the within-story correlation of the directional pairs $(A^{(1)}_{i,t}, A^{(2)}_{i,t})$ and  $r_i^{(2 \rightarrow 1)}$ is the corresponding correlation of the directional pairs $(A^{(2)}_{i,t}, A^{(1)}_{i,t+1})$

Directional asymmetry is then a measure of how much an agent immediately adapts to its counterpart in terms of sentiment. Since human-human and AI-AI conditions have arbitrary directions, we use absolute asymmetry, as negative and positive asymmetry correlations would otherwise cancel each other out.    

As an additional modeling metric we measure marginal sentiment push for a turn $T_t$, by calculating it as the absolute difference between the projection of the turn $v_t$ and the projection of the full conversation context up to and including the turn: $v({C_t, T_t})$, which yields

\begin{equation}
    |\Delta v_t|
    =
    \left|
    v(C_t, T_t) - v(C_t)
    \right|
\end{equation}
This measure aims to take the context of inputs into account, when estimating the sentiment contribution of a turn. 

\subsection{Novelty, transience and resonance}
For narrative influence, we use information-theoretic measures of discourse influence, namely Novelty, Transience and Resonance. Novelty measures the divergence of a turn from prior context, representing the introduction of novel/surprising material to the story. Transience measures the predictive effect of a turn on the immediately following story contribution. Resonance measures the relationship between the two, expressing whether that departure remains predictive of what follows.

\textbf{Novelty}: Which actor introduces new material?
\textbf{Transience}: How much does new material fail to persist?
\textbf{Resonance}: Whose novelty matters for the future of the story?

\paragraph{Suprisal}
%Single token surprisal 
We build on the established theory of surprisal-based novelty, transience, and resonance from \cite{fundal2026directionalalignmentnarrativeagency}, and expand the methodological paradigm of transience specifically. The single surprisal score of a token $w_j$ is computed as the negative log-probability of $w_j$ given the preceding tokens in a defined context window $w_{<j}$. The scores were computed using the open-source base model google/gemma-4-31b.
 \begin{equation}
s(w_j) = -\log_2 p(w_j \mid w_{<j})
\end{equation}

%Turn-level surprisal:
The surprisal score of turn-level inputs $\bar{s}(T_t \mid C_t)$ is then computed as the mean surprisal score of the tokens in a turn $T_t$ . Each token is conditioned on the preceding context $C_t$ and the preceding tokens within the turn $w_{t,<j}$.

\begin{equation}
\bar{s}(T_t \mid C_t)
=
\frac{1}{n_t}
\sum_{j=1}^{n_t}
-\log_2 p(w_{t,j} \mid C_t, w_{t,<j})
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Novelty}
We then define Novelty and Transience by calculating excess surprisal of the current turn given prior context, relative to a context-free baseline.

This can be interpreted as the negative of a model-based PMI estimate between the preceding context and the current turn. The contextual score is computed using all preceding tokens in the story $C_t$. The baseline score is calculated as the surprisal score of the turn from the model's beginning-of-sequence token (BOS). Formally: 
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Novelty}_t = \bar{s}(T_t \mid C_t) - \bar{s}(T_t \mid \mathrm{BOS})
\end{equation}

A very negative score indicates low novelty, whereas a less negative score indicates high novelty.
\paragraph{Transience}
We expand on the definition of transience from \cite{fundal2026directionalalignmentnarrativeagency}. Instead of regarding transience only as the isolated total predictive utility of a turn $s_t$, regardless of the prior context, we now measure the marginal contribution of $s_t$ given previous context to predict subsequent turns. This is obtained by conditioning on the past and asking how much the current turn increases surprise of the future turn \textbf{given} what has already been established. Formally, we transition from (i) to (ii):

\begin{equation}
    (i) \ \mathrm{Transience}_t=\bar{s}(F_{t+1}\mid T_t)-\bar{s}(F_{t+1}\mid BOS)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
(ii) \
\mathrm{Transience}_t
=
\bar{s}(F_{t+1} \mid C_t, T_t)
-
\bar{s}(F_{t+1} \mid C_t)
\end{equation}

where $F_{t+1}$ denotes the immediately following counterpart turn. This facilitates a measure of what the turn carries into the future narrative, in addition to what has already been established. High transience then indicates that $T_t$ made the future more surprising compared to the future without $T_t$ . If the future is more surprising given $T_t$, the turn did not have a lasting impact on the subsequent narrative. A very negative transience score indicates that the future integrated the turn, whereas a less negative score indicates that the turn was not predictive of the future, and a positive (rare) score indicates that the turn was disruptive for future discourse, as including it made the future harder to predict. 

%Resoncance
Finally, we define resonance as the net gain of novelty on future discourse (rephrase)
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Resonance}_t = \mathrm{Novelty}_t - \mathrm{Transience}_t
\end{equation}
Resonance is thus an expression of how much a contribution sticks in the future relative to how suprising it is. Stronger future uptake increases resonance, and weaker uptake dampens it.

If an agent suddenly introduces a dragon into the story and the counterpart continues to integrate the dragon in their turn, that original dragon input will yield high novelty (suddenly introducing a dragon), low transience (the turn about the dragon reduces surprise of the future turn), resulting in high resonance (high novelty - low transience). 

\paragraph{Modeling}
We model differences in agent influence asymmetry between conditions on two levels. The first level is the absolute magnitude of asymmetry for each metric $\overline{m}$ (novelty, transience, and resonance)
\begin{equation}
    A_m(S_i)
    =
    \left|
    \bar{m}^{(1)}_i
    -
    \bar{m}^{(2)}_i
    \right|
\end{equation}
where $m \in \{\mathrm{Novelty}, \mathrm{Transience}, \mathrm{Resonance}\}$ and $\bar{m}^{(a)}_i$ is the mean value of metric $m$ for agent $a$ in story $S_i$.

The second level models novel narrative influence as resonance predicted by novelty with an agent interaction effect. 
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{resonance} \sim \texttt{novelty} * \texttt{agent} + (1 \mid \texttt{conversation\_id})
\end{equation}
We then compute the slope asymmetry magnitude as the absolute difference between the novelty-to-resonance slopes of the two agents within a story: 

\begin{equation}
    A_{\mathrm{influence}}(S_i)
    =
    \left|
    \beta^{(1)}_{\mathrm{nov},i}
    -
    \beta^{(2)}_{\mathrm{nov},i}
    \right|
\end{equation}

\subsection{Semantic distance}  
Lastly, we add a semantic distance test that evaluates asymmetry as the cosine distance between each adjacent turn between agents. Since turns are alternating, each distance value $D_t$ represents local semantic asymmetry between the two agents in a turn. 
\begin{equation}
    D_t
    =
    1
    -
    \frac{
    e_t^{(1)} \cdot e_t^{(2)}
    }{
    \|e_t^{(1)}\| \, \|e_t^{(2)}\|
    }
\end{equation}

This was done both on interaction-level and aggregated on story-level.

\begin{equation}
    D(S_i)
    =
    \frac{1}{n_i}
    \sum_{t=1}^{n_i} D_t
\end{equation}

\subsection{Surface-level metrics}

In addition to the existing framework, we add an analysis paradigm that measures difference in the levels of asymmetry  in surface-level metrics, across conditions \cite{surface-level}. 

We use the following five metrics as a base for linguistic profiling:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Word count, 
    \item Sentence length, 
    \item Lexical diversity, 
    \item Readability
    \item Coherence
\end{enumerate}

For each story, we calculate the asymmetry magnitude $A_m$ as the absolute difference between agents for a given surface-level metric $m$
\begin{equation}
    A_m(S_i)
    =
    \left|
    m(A^{(1)}_i)
    -
    m(A^{(2)}_i)
    \right|
\end{equation}
To obtain a combined linguistic profile, we stack each asymmetry magnitude as a 5-dimensional vector, yielding a $209x5$ matrix.
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
A_{\mathrm{wc}}(S_1) & A_{\mathrm{sl}}(S_1) & A_{\mathrm{ld}}(S_1) & A_{\mathrm{read}}(S_1) & A_{\mathrm{coh}}(S_1) \\
A_{\mathrm{wc}}(S_2) & A_{\mathrm{sl}}(S_2) & A_{\mathrm{ld}}(S_2) & A_{\mathrm{read}}(S_2) & A_{\mathrm{coh}}(S_2) \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\
A_{\mathrm{wc}}(S_{209}) & A_{\mathrm{sl}}(S_{209}) & A_{\mathrm{ld}}(S_{209}) & A_{\mathrm{read}}(S_{209}) & A_{\mathrm{coh}}(S_{209})
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Where each column represents the absolute asymmetry for a specific surface metric:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $A_{wc}$: Word Count
    \item $A_{sl}$: Sentence Length
    \item $A_{ld}$: Lexical Diversity
    \item $A_{read}$: Readability (Flesch Reading Ease)
    \item $A_{coh}$: Coherence (First-Order Coherence)
\end{itemize}

In order to test the differences in asymmetry profiles between conditions, we implement a PERMANOVA test with 9999 permutations, using the Euclidean distance between all possible asymmetry profiles.

\subsection{Narrative agency metric comparison}
Humans are the main source of narrative novelty and resonance in Human--LLM writing (Novelty=-1.818; Transience=-1..022; Resonance=-0.796) compared to LLMs (Novelty=-2.640; Transience=-0.566; Resonance=-2.048). Human-authored turns showed higher novelty and resonance than LLM-authored turns, indicating that humans more often introduce material that remains active in subsequent narrative context.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{latex/figures/Novelty_Transience_Resonance_Distributions_Agent.png}
  \caption{.}
  \label{fig:novelty_metrics}
\end{figure}
Both agents showed a strong positive novelty--resonance relationship, indicating that more surprising turns tends to remain more active in subsequent narrative context. This relationship is stronger for human-authored turns than for LLM-authored turns. However, where prior work showed a significant interaction effect on agents, this was reduced to a trend level result given the new gemma-4 model and the redefined transience computation (results).

The cross-condition slot-slope asymmetry test, only showed trend level indications that the novelty-to-resonance coupling differed between slots within conditions. (Human--AI: $A_{\mathrm{influence}}=0.031$, $p=.410$), (Human--Human: $A_{\mathrm{influence}}=0.046$, $p=.489$), (AI--AI: $A_{\mathrm{influence}}=0.008$, $p=.831$).

\paragraph{Cross condition narrative influence}
The aggregate metric comparisons show distinct differences between LLM-LLM stories and human involved stories. AI--AI stories had lower novelty, transience, and resonance than Human--AI and Human--Human stories, while Human--AI and Human--Human were descriptively closer at the aggregate level. LLMs are in this relative sense predictable, but also very adaptable to their LLM partner, and when paired with one another produces highly predictable, frictionless stories. \subsection{Human-AI influence asymmetry}
Metric level slot asymmetry was largest in Human--AI stories. Per-story slot gaps were higher for Human--AI than the other two conditions for novelty (mean $A_m=0.828$, 95\% CI $[0.732,0.926]$), transience (mean $A_m=0.484$, 95\% CI $[0.432,0.537]$), and resonance (mean $A_m=1.290$, 95\% CI $[1.149,1.432]$). Planned contrasts comparing Human--AI against the average of Human--Human and AI--AI were significant for all three metrics ( $p<.001$) as well.

\subsection{General dynamic influence }
Finally, the pooled novelty--resonance model showed positive slopes in all conditions, with the strongest coupling in Human--AI stories (slope $=1.217$, 95\% CI $[1.184,1.250]$), followed by Human--Human (slope $=1.066$, 95\% CI $[1.003,1.128]$) and AI--AI (slope $=0.956$, 95\% CI $[0.913,0.999]$). The Human--Human and AI--AI slopes were both significantly flatter than the Human--AI slope.

\subsection{Human-LLM co-writing is semantically asymmetric}
Adjacent turn-to-turn semantic distance differed by condition. Human--AI stories had the highest adjacent semantic distance (mean $=0.316$), followed by AI--AI (mean $=0.296$) and Human--Human (mean $=0.287$). In the mixed model, Human--AI distances were larger than both Human--Human ($\Delta=0.0299$, $p<.0001$) and AI--AI ($\Delta=0.0198$,  $p<.0001$), while the Human--Human versus AI--AI contrast was weaker ($p=.0857$). The planned contrast against the average of the two same-type conditions was also significant ($\Delta=0.0248$, $p<.0001$).

\section{Discussion}
Treating co-writing as an unfolding interaction, our work has provided a finer grained account of creativity and coordination in open ended text generation. We have revealed systematic patterns in how narrative influence and alignment unfold across conditions. 

Together these findings suggest that Human-AI co-writing is a distinct interactional regime characterized by asymmetric agency and adaptive alignment, with properties the differ from both Human-Human and LLM-LLM co-writing. 

Our approach improves over prior evaluations by enabling a fine-grained analysis of co-writing dynamics and capturing both narrative innovation and adaptive behavior. 

Our approach contributes to NLP research on human-AI collaboration by modeling narrative development as an emergence distribution of agency across interactive writers, as well as to creative co-writing research in general ...
